It started with looking for Yamamoto.

In fact, we weren’t looking for Yamamoto.

We had gone to Tokyo for my niece’s quinceañera. It was a trip much more about anime and Japan than jazz. My cousin Toño wanted to buy Tsuyoshi Yamamoto records for his father, who is a fan, and one night he suggested we go hear jazz.

So we did the most naïve thing possible: we showed up at Blue Note Tokyo expecting to get tickets that night.

There weren’t any.

That night we started looking into Tokyo’s jazz scene and found a small club in Roppongi: Jazz House ALFIE.

The next morning I sent Toño the schedule. I thought I understood that someone named Yamamoto was playing that week.

“Tsuyoshi Yamamoto?”

For a few hours we thought we had stumbled into absurd luck. We also thought we might never get a reservation: ALFIE took reservations by phone, in Japanese, and was closed on Mondays.

On Tuesday we found our mistake. Yamamoto had played a few weeks earlier.

We went anyway.

When we arrived and I heard “Osorio-san,” we knew we had found a special place. ALFIE was small, intimate, and extraordinary. That night we heard Honami with Naoto Suzuki, Akiyoshi Shimizu, and Yuto Maseki. We loved it.

Then someone at ALFIE told us that Yamamoto played there about every three months.

“We have to come back.”

Going back to Tokyo to find Yamamoto is, obviously, a rather disproportionate excuse to go to a concert.

That is precisely the charm of it.

Since then the search has kept drifting toward other musicians, records, clubs, and cities.

For now, we keep looking for Yamamoto. After that, we’ll see where the music takes us.
