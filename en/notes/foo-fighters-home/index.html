Home Sweet Fucking Home

Foo Fighters, thirty-one years, and what a first concert can mean later

Foo Fighters · Nationals Park · Washington, D.C. · 17 August 2026

“It’s good to be back home.”

Dave Grohl had barely stepped onto the stage.

It could have been ordinary concert talk. Touring bands have been telling every city that tonight is special for as long as touring bands have existed. But Grohl kept coming back to the same idea, and it soon became clear he was not just working the crowd.

Washington was not another stop.

Grohl was not born here. But this is where he became a musician. Throughout the night he talked about Alexandria, Arlington, garages, friends, and people who had known him long before Foo Fighters. He dedicated “These Days” to family and old friends in the crowd. At another point he looked out across Nationals Park and said:

“It’s a beautiful night for a show in the city where I learned to play music.”

That was the way into the night.

Grohl kept pulling on that thread.

Foo Fighters have been playing for thirty-one years.

At this point they could walk onstage, play the hits, and call it a night. The catalog is more than deep enough: “My Hero,” “Learn to Fly,” “The Pretender,” “Best of You,” “Everlong.” They could let the crowd do half the work and still send everyone home happy.

That is not what they did.

For three hours at Nationals Park, Foo Fighters sounded like a band that still wanted to play these songs, not one simply running through the catalog.

The opening run was vicious: “All My Life,” “The Pretender,” “Times Like These,” “Rope,” “Stacked Actors.” There was almost no warm-up. Grohl still throws himself into these songs with a ridiculous amount of force for someone who has been playing them for decades.

And the band sounds heavy right now.

Ilan Rubin has a lot to do with that.

He does not play like Taylor Hawkins, and that is probably the best thing he could do. Taylor’s absence is still there and always will be. Rubin does not try to erase it or fill the space by copying him. He plays differently: drier, harder, more precise.

The old songs gain something from it.

“The Pretender” sounds enormous. “Rope” gets its edge back. “All My Life,” after all those years on the radio, remembers that it was always a fairly violent song.

After thirty years, any band can end up becoming its own tribute act. Eventually the audience stops caring quite as much about what you are doing now and just wants to hear what it already knows.

Foo Fighters still resist that.

Of course there were moments that only work at this scale.

On “My Hero,” the stadium eventually sang louder than Grohl. During “Best of You,” he could stop singing and let the crowd take over. And “Everlong,” after three decades of radio, concerts, covers, and memories that no longer belong only to the band, still arrived intact.

After three hours, fireworks could have been too much.

They were not.

I had seen Foo Fighters once before.

Nine months earlier, in Monterrey.

They played the stadium at Tec de Monterrey, on the same campus where I studied and began putting together much of my adult life.

I was not born in Monterrey and I did not grow up there. But I lived in the city for six years. I studied economics there, made friends I still have, and met the person with whom I would later build a family.

I went to that show with a friend from those years.

Less than a year later I was seeing them again, this time in Washington.

I was not born here either. But I have lived in this area long enough that coming back to Washington after a trip really does feel like coming home.

This time I went with my wife and daughter, along with two close friends here and their teenage daughter.

It was my daughter’s first concert.

That part was deliberate.

What I had not expected was that Grohl would spend so much of the night talking about coming home.

And that brought me back to my own first concert.

January 14, 1995.

My father took us from Tlaxcala to Mexico City to see the Rolling Stones on the Voodoo Lounge tour.

Caifanes opened.

I was fourteen. I would turn fifteen on February 4.

I knew perfectly well that the Rolling Stones were huge. I was not too young to understand that.

What I did not have was any real measure for what more than thirty years of a band’s life meant.

It was the Stones’ first concert in Mexico and their first in Latin America. I did not know then that I had walked into one of those nights that takes on a different scale with time.

At fourteen, thirty years is almost an abstraction.

You know you are watching a legend. You can see it in the size of the place, in the crowd, in songs that seemed to exist before you did. But you do not know what it feels like for three decades to pass.

At fourteen, you have nothing to measure thirty years against. Now I do.

Someone takes you to a concert.

The lights go down.

A band walks out.

And much later you understand a little better what you saw.

Years later, when it was time to choose my daughter’s first concert, some of that reference point was still there.

I was not looking for “the new Rolling Stones.” There are no new Rolling Stones. I did not want to turn her first concert into a lesson in rock history either. That would have ruined it.

I only wanted a band with enough history behind it that seeing them now might mean something different twenty or thirty years from now.

Foo Fighters felt right.

Then I looked at the dates.

When my father took me to see the Rolling Stones on January 14, 1995, Foo Fighters had never played a concert.

Dave Grohl turned 26 that same day.

He had already recorded the songs that would become the first Foo Fighters album, but the band had not yet played in front of an audience.

Their first performance, still private, came a few weeks later in Seattle.

The first public show was February 23 at the Jambalaya Club in Arcata, California.

Forty days after mine.

That day I was watching a band with more than three decades behind it.

Foo Fighters had not yet played live.

There was no reason those two facts should ever end up in the same story.

That same year, my own life changed quite a bit.

A few months later I left Tlaxcala and moved to Pachuca, the city where I had been born, to live with my grandparents and attend high school.

I lived with them for three years. I reconnected with much of my extended family, made new friends, and started a far more independent chapter of my life than the one I had known before.

Looking back at 1995 now, I see a lot of things beginning at roughly the same time.

Back then they were just separate things.

A first concert.

A move.

A new school.

New friends.

And, somewhere far away from any of it, a band beginning to play.

None of it seemed connected.

Thirty-one years later, those lines crossed.

On August 17, 2026, Foo Fighters were onstage at Nationals Park.

Thirty-one years had passed since those first shows in tiny rooms.

Pachuca.

Monterrey.

Washington.

They do not mean the same thing.

Pachuca is where I was born and where much of my family is rooted. Monterrey is where my adult life began. Washington is where I built the life I have now.

Grohl did not stop to define exactly what he meant by “home” either.

He did not have to.

He was playing a few miles from the garages, clubs, streets, and people he had been talking about all night.

“It’s a beautiful night for a show in the city where I learned to play music.”

And he kept playing.

And there was my ten-year-old daughter, watching her first concert.

She does not need to know that Foo Fighters have been doing this for thirty-one years, or that my first concert happened forty days before theirs, or why I chose this particular band for that particular night.

I did not understand all of that in 1995 either.

That came later.

Maybe it will for her.

Maybe not.

You never know, while a night is happening, which ones will end up mattering.

Sometimes being there is enough.
