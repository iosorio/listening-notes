Home Sweet Fucking Home

Foo Fighters, thirty-one years, and what a first concert can mean later

Foo Fighters · Nationals Park · Washington, D.C. · 17 August 2026

“It’s good to be back home.”

Dave Grohl had barely stepped onto the stage.

It could have been ordinary concert talk. Touring bands have been telling every city that tonight is special for as long as touring bands have existed. But Grohl kept returning to the idea, and it soon became clear he was not just working the crowd.

Washington was not another stop.

Grohl was not born here. But this is where he became a musician. Throughout the night he talked about Alexandria, Arlington, garages, friends, and people who had known him long before Foo Fighters. He dedicated “These Days” to family and old friends in the crowd. At another point he looked out across Nationals Park and said:

“It’s a beautiful night for a show in the city where I learned to play music.”

That was the way into the night.

Foo Fighters have been playing for thirty-one years. At this point they could walk onstage, play the hits, and call it a night. The catalog is more than deep enough: “My Hero,” “Learn to Fly,” “The Pretender,” “Best of You,” “Everlong.” They could let the crowd do half the work and still send everyone home happy.

That is not what they did.

For three hours at Nationals Park, Foo Fighters sounded like a band that still wanted to play these songs, not one simply running through the catalog.

The opening run was vicious: “All My Life,” “The Pretender,” “Times Like These,” “Rope,” “Stacked Actors.” There was almost no warm-up. Grohl still throws himself into these songs with a ridiculous amount of force for someone who has been playing them for decades.

And the band sounds heavy right now.

Ilan Rubin has a lot to do with that. He does not play like Taylor Hawkins, and that is probably the best thing he could do. Taylor’s absence is still there. Rubin does not try to fill the space by copying him; he plays differently: drier, harder, more precise.

The old songs gain something from it. “The Pretender” sounds enormous. “Rope” gets its edge back. “All My Life,” after all those years on the radio, remembers that it was always a fairly violent song.

After thirty years, any band can end up becoming its own tribute act. Foo Fighters still resist that.

Of course there were moments that only work at this scale. On “My Hero,” the stadium eventually sang louder than Grohl. During “Best of You,” he could stop singing and let the crowd take over. And “Everlong,” after three decades of radio, concerts, covers, and memories that no longer belong only to the band, still arrived intact.

After three hours, fireworks could have been too much.

They were not.

I had seen Foo Fighters once before.

Nine months earlier, in Monterrey, they played the stadium at Tec de Monterrey, on the same campus where I studied and began putting together much of my adult life. I lived in that city for six years. I made friends there I still have and met the person who would later become my wife.

I went to that show with a friend from those years.

Less than a year later I was seeing them again, this time in Washington, the other city that eventually began to feel like coming home.

This time I went with my wife and daughter, along with two friends and their teenage daughter.

It was my daughter’s first concert.

That part was deliberate. I wanted her to start with a band that had enough history behind it that seeing them now might mean something different twenty or thirty years from now.

What I had not expected was that Grohl would spend so much of the night talking about coming home.

And that brought me back to my own first concert.

January 14, 1995.

My father took us to Mexico City to see the Rolling Stones on the Voodoo Lounge tour.

Caifanes opened.

I was fourteen. I would turn fifteen on February 4.

I knew perfectly well that the Rolling Stones were huge. What I did not have was any real measure for what more than thirty years of a band’s life meant.

It was the Stones’ first concert in Mexico and their first in Latin America. I did not know then that I had walked into one of those nights that takes on a different scale with time.

At fourteen, thirty years is almost an abstraction. You know you are watching a legend: you can see it in the size of the place, in the crowd, in songs that seemed to exist before you did. But you do not know what it feels like for three decades to pass.

At fourteen, you have nothing to measure thirty years against. Now I do.

Someone takes you to a concert. The lights go down. A band walks out. Much later you understand a little better what you saw.

I was not looking for “the new Rolling Stones” for my daughter. There are no new Rolling Stones. I did not want to turn her first concert into a lesson in rock history either.

Foo Fighters simply felt right.

Then I looked at the dates.

When my father took me to see the Rolling Stones on January 14, 1995, Foo Fighters had never played a concert.

Dave Grohl turned 26 that same day.

He had already recorded the songs that would become the first Foo Fighters album, but the band had not yet played in front of an audience. Their first public show was February 23 at the Jambalaya Club in Arcata, California.

Forty days after mine.

That day I was watching a band with more than three decades behind it. The band I would choose thirty-one years later for my daughter’s first concert had not yet played live.

There is not much to explain there.

On August 17, 2026, Grohl was at Nationals Park, a few miles from the garages, clubs, streets, and people he had been talking about all night.

“It’s a beautiful night for a show in the city where I learned to play music.”

And there was my ten-year-old daughter, watching her first concert.

She does not need to know that Foo Fighters have been doing this for thirty-one years, or that my first concert happened forty days before theirs, or why I chose this particular band for that particular night.

I did not understand all of that in 1995 either.

That came later.

Maybe it will for her.

Maybe not.

You never know, while a night is happening, which ones will end up mattering.

Sometimes being there is enough.
